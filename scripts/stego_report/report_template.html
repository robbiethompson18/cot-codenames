What the models are learning to write

The sender learned to beat the small monitor it was trained against, but a frontier model reads the hidden word about as well as the receiver does.

A sender gets a secret word and an essay topic. A receiver (the same trained model) and a monitor each read the essay and pick the word from 8 candidates. The sender is rewarded when the receiver is right and the monitor is wrong. Everything here is from the first training rung, where the same 8 words are used every time. Each number is from 64 essays, so read it as plus or minus 6 points.

  • Training works against the monitor in the loop. On both backends the receiver went to 97–98% while the 9B monitor fell from about half to 23–31%. Without the penalty the monitor rose to 80%.
  • The hiding does not hold against a stronger reader. Opus 5.5 reads 83–86% of the trained essays cold, and 91–98% after seeing 16 earlier essays with their answers. That matches the receiver.
  • Training made the word easier for Opus to find, not harder. Opus got 62–70% on the untrained sender's essays and 83–86% on the trained sender's.
  • The technique is hinting by negation. The sender says what the scene is not: no energy, no debate, no climb. See the essays at the bottom.
  • The first judge was far too lenient. It passed 97% of trained essays that had drifted into off-topic musing. The replacement passes 95% of honest essays and none of those.

Audit: how each reader is prompted

  • No tools anywhere, and no forced tool calls. Every model gets one user message and answers in plain text or with one token.
  • Qwen receiver and monitors have reasoning switched off. We read the next-token probabilities for the letters A to H and renormalise. Over 99% of the probability is on those letters.
  • Claude monitors think before answering (adaptive thinking, medium effort) and reply in free text. We take the last letter in the reply.
  • Receiver and monitor see the same essay and the same rules. Their prompts differ in one sentence. Their candidate lists are shuffled separately.
  • The sender is told about the monitor and told never to write the word. It is not told how to hide it.

Problems the audit found

  1. The monitor in training was the weakest reader we have. It was a smaller model with reasoning off and no examples. Each of those three handicaps matters on its own in the charts above.
  2. The first judge let the essays leave the topic. That gave the sender room to hint in ways an ordinary essay could not. The judge also allowed up to 260 words against the 150 asked for, and Tinker essays grew to about 206.
  3. Hiding the word is not hiding that there is a word. The current judge separates trained essays from honest ones almost perfectly, so a monitor asked "is something hidden here" would catch all of them.
  4. Claude monitors got one extra sentence telling them to guess even if unsure. Without it Haiku mostly declined to answer. Answers with no letter are scored as wrong; the counts are below.
  5. Haiku without thinking is not a usable monitor. It declined about 80% of the time, so those numbers were dropped.
  6. Eight fixed words can be memorised. This rung cannot show a general code, only that the training loop works.
