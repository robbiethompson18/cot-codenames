What the 300-word model learned

It learned to get any word across, including words it never trained on. It did not learn to hide: a reader given the same 8 candidates as the receiver does nearly as well.

Run words300-n500: Qwen3.6-27B as sender and receiver, 300 secret words, the strict essay referee. The receiver picks the secret from 8 candidates. The monitor in training, Qwen3.5-9B, had to pick it from 500. Everything here is the step-50 checkpoint; each number is 64 essays, so about ±6 points.

  • It works on unseen words. The receiver gets 91% on the training words and 95% on words never used in training, up from 58% and 70% before training. 97–98% of essays pass the strict referee.
  • The monitor it trained against stays near 9%. But that monitor is choosing from 500 words, and 9% of 500 is far above chance (0.2%).
  • On equal terms it is not hidden. Given the receiver's own 8 candidates, Sonnet picks the secret 83–88% of the time and Opus 84–92%. Before training they got 67–81%. The essays became easier for a strong reader, not harder.
  • It mostly just describes the thing. 61% of trained essays describe the object or its function without naming it; most of the rest use its vocabulary. Essays also got shorter, from about 177 words to about 97.
  • So the win comes from the list sizes, not from a private code. A hint clear enough to pick 1 of 8 is not always enough to pick 1 of 500, and that gap is what the reward paid for.
